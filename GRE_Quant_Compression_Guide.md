# GRE-Prep

---

## What This Is (and What It Isn’t)

This is not a study guide.

It’s a compression artifact.

The GRE Quantitative section does not test mathematical depth. It tests whether you can recognize common problem structures quickly and apply the correct procedure under time pressure. Most test-takers already know the underlying math. What they lack is fast recall, procedural confidence, and resistance to traps.

This document exists to compress the GRE Quant syllabus into a set of recognizable patterns, decision rules, and minimal procedures that can be executed quickly and reliably. If you are looking for theory, proofs, or comprehensive coverage, this is the wrong resource. If you want to stop burning time remembering how to solve things you already learned years ago, this is exactly the point.

How to Use This Document

Each section focuses on a specific class of GRE Quant problems and is structured around three questions:

1. What kind of question is this, really?

2. What is the fastest correct approach?

3. What mistake is the test trying to bait you into making?

You should read each section actively, then immediately drill that exact problem type until recognition becomes automatic. The act of constructing and refining these explanations is intentional: writing forces compression, and compression is what turns dormant knowledge into usable skill under time constraints.

This document is intentionally basic, incomplete and opinionated. It prioritizes frequency, speed, and return on effort over elegance or exhaustiveness. That is not a flaw. That is the point.

# Section 1
## 1. Integers and Number Properties

Integers and Number Properties form the backbone of the GRE Quant section. These questions rarely appear in isolation; instead, they are embedded inside algebra, word problems, and quantitative comparisons. The math itself is elementary, but the GRE exploits hesitation, forgotten rules, and over-computation.

The primary challenge in this category is not difficulty, but recall speed. Most incorrect answers come from failing to recognize which property matters, or from doing unnecessary arithmetic instead of applying a simple rule. This section focuses on rapid identification of number behavior and the shortest correct path to an answer.

### Divisibility rules

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you remember quick divisibility checks and can avoid long division or factorization.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “divisible by”

- “multiple of”

- “evenly divisible”

- “no remainder when divided by”

If you see these phrases, you should be thinking rules, not arithmetic.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

Which of the following numbers is divisible by $6$?

A) $4,212$

B) $4,215$

C) $4,218$

D) $4,221$

E) $4,224$


**Fast solution path**

A number is divisible by $6$ if and only if:

it is divisible by $2$ ($even$), and

divisible by $3$ (sum of digits divisible by $3$)

Check options quickly:

A) $4212$

Even. Digit sum $= 4 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 9$ → divisible by $3$ → yes

**Trap note**

The trap is checking only one condition. Many answers are $even$ but not divisible by $3$, or divisible by $3$ but $odd$.

**Takeaway**

When divisibility is mentioned, apply rules first. Division is a last resort.

**[Go to Divisibility Drills](Drills.md#divisibility-rules)**

### Prime numbers

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand what primes are, how they behave in factorization, and when not to treat $1$ as a prime.

Most mistakes here are definition errors, not math errors.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “prime”

- “unique prime factorization”

- “product of primes”

- “smallest / largest possible value”

If $1$ is anywhere near the problem, be alert.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

Which of the following numbers has exactly three distinct prime factors?

A) $18$

B) $20$

C) $24$

D) $30$

E) $36$

**Fast solution path**

Factor each number into primes, stopping as soon as the count is clear.

A) $18 = 2 × 3^2$ → primes: ${2, 3}$ → $2$ primes

B) $20 = 2^2 × 5$ → primes: ${2, 5}$ → $2$ primes

C) $24 = 2^3 × 3$ → primes: ${2, 3}$ → $2$ primes

D) $30 = 2 × 3 × 5$ → primes: ${2, 3, 5}$ → $3$ primes

Stop. You don’t need to check E.

Answer: D

**Trap note**

- Treating $1$ as a prime (it is not)

- Counting repeated primes more than once

- Fully factoring every option instead of stopping early

**Takeaway**

Prime problems are about structure, not computation. Factor only as far as needed.

**[Go to Prime number Drills](Drills.md#prime-numbers)**

---

### Factors and multiples

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand the relationship between numbers, not whether you can list factors exhaustively. 

These problems reward structural thinking and punish brute force.

**Fast recognition cues**

“factor of”

“multiple of”

“divides evenly”

“how many positive divisors”

“greatest / least possible value”

If you see “how many” combined with factors, pause before computing.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

How many positive factors does the number $72$ have?

**Fast solution path**

First, write the prime factorization:

$72 = 2^3 × 3^2$

To count factors:

Add $1$ to each exponent

Multiply the results

Number of positive factors

$= (3 + 1)(2 + 1)$

$= 4 × 3$

$= 12$

Answer: $12$

**Trap note**

Listing factors manually (slow and error-prone)

Forgetting to include $1$ and the number itself

Miscounting repeated prime powers

**Takeaway**

When asked for the number of factors, factor once and apply the exponent rule. Never list.

**[Go to Factors and multiples Drills](Drills.md#factors-and-multiples)**

### Even/odd behavior

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand how parity behaves under basic operations. These problems are not about numbers, they’re about patterns. 

The GRE expects you to reason symbolically instead of plugging in values blindly.

**Fast recognition cues**

“$even$”

“$odd$”

“$integer$”

“$sum$ / $difference$ / $product$”

Quantitative Comparison questions involving parity

If variables are involved and parity is mentioned, stop thinking numerically.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

If $𝑥$ is an $odd$ $integer$ and $𝑦$ is an $even$ $integer$, which of the following must be $even$?

A) $𝑥 + 𝑦$

B) $𝑥 − 𝑦$

C) $𝑥𝑦$

D) $𝑥^(2) + 𝑦$

E) $𝑥^(2) − 𝑦$

**Fast solution path**

Use parity rules:

- $odd + even = odd$

- $odd − even = odd$

- $odd × even = even$

- $odd^(2) = odd$

Check options:

A) $odd + even = odd$

B) $odd − even = odd$

C) $odd × even = even$ ← must be $even$

D) $odd + even = odd$

E) $odd − even = odd$

Answer: C

**Trap note**

- Plugging in random numbers instead of using parity rules

- Forgetting that odd² is still odd

- Overthinking simple operations

**Takeaway**

Parity problems are rule-based. Apply the rules once and move on. Plugging in values is slower and unnecessary.

**[Go to Even/odd behavior Drills](Drills.md#evenodd-behavior)**

### Remainders

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand division with remainders conceptually, not whether you can perform long division. The GRE loves remainder problems because they expose people who compute instead of reasoning.

Most of these reduce to:

“What happens when I rewrite this in the form: $divisor × quotient + remainder$?”

**Fast recognition cues**

- “$remainder$”

- “when $divided$ by”

- “leaves a $remainder$ of”

- “consecutive $integers$” combined with $division$

- Quantitative Comparison involving remainders

If a problem mentions $division$ but does not ask for a quotient, think $remainders$ immediately.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

When an $integer$ $𝑛$ is $divided$ by $5$, the $remainder$ is $2$. What is the remainder when 
$3𝑛$ is $divided$ by $5$?

**Fast solution path**

Rewrite 

$𝑛$ using the division algorithm:

$𝑛 = 5𝑘 + 2$

Multiply both sides by $3$:

$3𝑛 = 3(5𝑘 + 2) = 15𝑘 + 6$

Now $divide$ by $5$:

$15𝑘$ is $divisible$ by $5$

$6 ÷ 5$ leaves a remainder of $1$

Answer: $1$

**Trap note**

- Dividing the original number directly instead of rewriting it

- Forgetting to reduce the final value modulo the divisor

- Treating the remainder as if it scales linearly without checking

**Takeaway**

Always rewrite numbers as

$divisor × integer + remainder$.

Then manipulate that expression. Do not divide blindly.

**[Go to Remainders Drills](Drills.md#remainders)**

---

### Least common multiple (LCM)

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand how multiples align, not whether you can list them. LCM questions reward prime-factor structure and punish trial-and-error listing.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “least common multiple”

- “smallest positive integer divisible by”

- “when will they occur at the same time”

- word problems involving repeating cycles

If two or more numbers are involved and “least” appears, think prime factors.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

What is the least common multiple of $18$ and $24$?

**Fast solution path**

Prime factorize both numbers:

$18 = 2 × 3^2$

$24 = 2^3 × 3$

For the LCM:

Take the highest power of each prime

LCM $= 2^3 × 3^2$

$= 8 × 9$

$= 72$

Answer: $72$

**Trap note**

- Taking the product of the numbers unnecessarily

- Using the smallest exponents instead of the largest

- Listing multiples manually

**Takeaway**

For LCM, take every prime involved at its highest power. Never list multiples.

**[Go to Least common multiple (LCM) Drills](Drills.md#lcm)**

---

### Greatest common divisor (GCD)

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you can identify shared structure between numbers. GCD problems are the mirror image of LCM problems and should feel mechanically similar.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “greatest common divisor”

- “greatest factor”

- “largest integer that divides”

- simplifying fractions or ratios

If simplification is mentioned, GCD is nearby.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

What is the greatest common divisor of $84$ and $126$?

**Fast solution path**

Prime factorize:

$84 = 2^2 × 3 × 7$

$126 = 2 × 3² × 7$

For the GCD:

Take the lowest power of each shared prime

GCD $= 2^1 × 3^1 × 7^1$
$= 42$

Answer: $42$

**Trap note**

- Including primes that don’t appear in both numbers

- Taking the highest powers instead of the lowest

- Confusing GCD with LCM under time pressure

**Takeaway**

For GCD, take only shared primes at their lowest powers.

**[Go to Greatest common divisor (GCD) Drills](Drills.md#gcd)**

### Units digit patterns

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you recognize repeating cycles instead of computing large powers. These problems are pure pattern recognition.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “units digit”

- “last digit”

- large exponents (especially powers)

- Quantitative Comparison involving powers

If the exponent looks large, do not compute.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:
What is the units digit of $7^{103}$?

**Fast solution path**

List the repeating pattern of units digits for powers of 7:

$7^{1} → 7$

$7^{2} → 9$

$7^{3} → 3$

$7^{4} → 1$

Pattern length $= 4$

Now reduce the exponent modulo $4$:

$103 ÷ 4$ leaves a remainder of $3$

So the units digit matches $7^{3}$:

$3$

Answer: $3$

**Trap note**

- Forgetting the cycle length

- Not reducing the exponent modulo the cycle

- Trying to compute powers directly

**Takeaway**

Units digit problems are cycle problems. Find the cycle, reduce the exponent, stop.

**[Go to Units digit patterns Drills](Drills.md#unit-digits)**

### Fraction simplification

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you can recognize common factors quickly and reduce expressions without overworking the arithmetic. These problems are about structural cancellation, not division.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “simplify”

- large numerator and denominator

- algebraic fractions

- common binomial factors

- numbers that obviously share factors (e.g., both divisible by $6$, $9$, $12$)

If both numerator and denominator look large, look for common structure first.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

Simplify:

$$\frac{84}{126}$$

**Fast solution path**

Factor both numbers:

$$84 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 7$$

$$126 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 7$$

Cancel common factors:

$$\frac{2^2 \times 3 \times 7}{2 \times 3^2 \times 7} = \frac{2}{3}$$

Answer:

$$\frac{2}{3}$$

**Trap note**

- Dividing blindly with a calculator

- Cancelling terms incorrectly in algebraic expressions

- Forgetting that cancellation only works across multiplication, not addition

**Takeaway**

Factor first. Cancel second. Divide last.

---

### Ratio translation to equations

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you can correctly translate ratios into algebra. Most mistakes come from misplacing variables or misreading order.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “ratio of”

- “$x$ is to $y$ as”

- “in the ratio”

- word problems involving proportional relationships

If a ratio is described in words, slow down before writing the equation.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

The ratio of $x$ to $y$ is $3:5$.

If $x + y = 40$, what is the value of $x$?

**Fast solution path**

Translate the ratio correctly:

$$𝑥 = 3𝑘$$

$$𝑦 = 5𝑘$$

Now use the sum:

$$3𝑘 + 5𝑘 = 40$$

$$8𝑘 = 40$$

$$𝑘 = 5$$

Therefore:

$$𝑥 = 3𝑘 = 15$$

Answer: $15$

**Trap note**

- Writing $\frac{x}{y} = \frac{5}{3}$ instead of $\frac{3}{5}$

- Plugging 3 and 5 directly as values instead of multiples

- Forgetting to use a common multiplier

**Takeaway**

Ratios describe proportional structure. Always assign a multiplier.

---

### Percent increase / decrease

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand percent change as a multiplicative adjustment, not additive intuition. These questions punish sloppy thinking.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “percent increase”

- “percent decrease”

- “after a 20% increase”

- “discount”

- “tax”

- “markup”

If percent change appears, think multiplication.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

A price of $200 is increased by $15%$.

What is the new price?

**Fast solution path**

Percent increase means multiply by:

$$1+0.15=1.15$$

So:

$$200×1.15=230$$

Answer: $230$

**Alternative mental method**

$10%$ of $200$ is $20$

$5%$ is $10$

Total increase is $30$

$200 + 30 = 230$

Same result. Faster for round numbers.

**Trap note**

- Adding $15$ instead of $15%$

- Forgetting that a percent decrease uses multiplication by $(1 - r)$

- Confusing percent change with percent of

**Takeaway**

Percent change is multiplication, not addition.

---

### Percent change vs percent of

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you can distinguish between finding a percentage of a number and finding the percent change between two numbers. These are conceptually different, and the GRE exploits that confusion.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “what percent of”

- “by what percent did”

- “percent change”

- “increase from ___ to ___”

- “decrease from ___ to ___”

If two numbers are being compared, think percent change formula.

If only one number is given and a percentage is requested, think percent of.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

A quantity increases from $50$ to $65$.
By what percent did it increase?

**Fast solution path**

Percent change formula:

$$\frac{new - original}{original}$$

So:

$$\frac{65 - 50}{50} = \frac{15}{50} = 0.3$$

Convert to percent:

$$0.3 = 30%$$

Answer: $30%$

**Contrast example (percent of)**

What is $30%$ of $50$?

$$0.3 \times 50 = 15$$

Different question. Different structure.

**Trap note**

- Dividing by the new value instead of the original

- Confusing “percent of” with “percent change”

- Forgetting that percent change is relative to the starting value

**Takeaway**

Percent change compares two numbers. Percent of applies to one number.

### Weighted averages (informal)

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you understand that averages must account for quantity. Most mistakes come from averaging averages.

**Fast recognition cues**

- “average of”

- groups of different sizes

- combining two averages

- mixture or grade problems

If group sizes differ, a simple average is wrong.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

A class of 10 students has an average score of $80$.
Another class of 20 students has an average score of $90$.
What is the average score of all 30 students?

**Fast solution path**

Convert averages back to totals.

First class total:

$$10×80=800$$

Second class total:

$$20×90=1800$$

Combined total:

$$800+1800=2600$$

Divide by total students:

$$\frac{2600}{30} = 86.\overline{6}$$

Answer:

$$86.\overline{6}$$

**Trap note**

- Taking $(80 + 90)/2 = 85$ (incorrect)

- Forgetting to weight by group size

- Rounding too early

**Takeaway**

When combining averages, return to totals first.

---

### Converting between fractions, decimals, and percents

**What the GRE is really testing:**

Whether you can move fluidly between representations without slowing down. This is foundational for Data Interpretation and percent problems.

**Fast recognition cues**

mixed representations in answer choices

comparison questions

repeating decimals

awkward fractions like $\frac{3}{8}$ or $\frac{7}{12}$

If formats differ, convert to the simplest common form.

**Canonical example problem**

Example:

Which is greatest?

A) $\frac{3}{8}$

B) $0.36$

C) $35\%$

D) $\frac{7}{20}$

E) $0.34$

**Fast solution path**

Convert everything to decimals.

$$\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$$

$$35\% = 0.35$$

$$\frac{7}{20} = 0.35$$

So compare:

A) $0.375$

B) $0.36$

C) $0.35$

D) $0.35$

E) $0.34$

Largest is:

$$0.375$$

Answer: A

**Trap note**

Converting everything when only one conversion is needed

Mishandling percent-to-decimal conversion

Forgetting common fraction equivalents (e.g., $1/8 = 0.125$)

**Takeaway**

Convert to the format that makes comparison easiest. Do not mix forms mid-calculation.
